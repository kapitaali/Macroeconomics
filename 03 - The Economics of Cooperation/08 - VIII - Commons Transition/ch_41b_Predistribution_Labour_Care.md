# Chapter 41b: Predistribution and the Labour Commons
## Valuing Work, Time, and Care in a Cooperative-Regenerative Economy

> *"Poverty is not a natural condition. It is manufactured — produced and reproduced through

## 41b.1 The Predistribution Question

The cooperative-regenerative framework has, up to this point, treated the distribution of income
as something that the Shapley value corrects after production has occurred. Chapter 32 proved that
cooperative allocation compresses the Gini coefficient relative to competitive allocation; Chapter 6
showed that the Shapley value distributes cooperative surplus fairly across players; and Chapter 34's
analysis of the Mondragon cooperative documented what a half-century of cooperative wage policy
looks like in practice — wage ratios of 6:1 where comparable conventional firms run at 50:1 or
higher.

But there is a prior question that all of these analyses leave implicit: *how does the primary
distribution — the distribution of income before cooperative allocation, before taxation, before
any corrective mechanism — come to look the way it does?* The answer is institutional. Primary
distribution is the product of labour law, property rights, collective bargaining coverage, minimum
wage legislation, the governance of working time, and — crucially, and almost entirely invisibly
— the valuation or non-valuation of care labour. These institutions determine who earns what
before any cooperative game is played, before any tax is levied, before any transfer is made.

This is the predistribution question. It was formalised in the political economy literature by Hacker
(2011) and developed in the context of post-growth economics by the European post-growth policy
research programmes of the 2020s. The UN Special Rapporteur's 2026 Roadmap for Eradicating
Poverty Beyond Growth elevated it to the centre of the post-growth policy agenda, with an entire
pillar — Pillar 2, on Labour, Care and Economic Democracy — devoted to the proposition that
poverty and inequality cannot be adequately addressed by redistribution alone if the primary
distribution that redistribution corrects is itself deeply unjust.

This chapter formalises three predistributive instruments that the cooperative-regenerative framework
requires but has not yet formally grounded: the employment guarantee, working time reduction, and
the Shapley extension to care labour. Together, they constitute what we call the Cooperative
Predistribution Equilibrium.

**Definition 41b.1 (Predistribution).** Let $\mathcal{E}$ be the set of admissible economic
institutions — labour law, property rights, enterprise governance norms, care recognition systems.
For each $E \in \mathcal{E}$, let $F(E) : \mathcal{L} \times \mathcal{K} \to \Delta(\mathbb{R}^n)$
denote the primary income distribution generated by institutions $E$ over a population of $n$ agents
with labour endowments $\mathcal{L}$ and capital endowments $\mathcal{K}$. Predistribution is the
deliberate choice of $E \in \mathcal{E}$ to shape $F(E)$ directly — altering the rules under which
income is generated, rather than correcting the outcomes after the fact.

**Definition 41b.2 (Redistribution).** Given fixed institutions $E^0$, redistribution is a
tax-transfer schedule $\tau : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ applied to primary incomes
$y = F(E^0)(\ell, k)$, producing post-tax incomes $y' = y - \tau(y)$, subject to the
budget constraint $\sum_i \tau_i(y) = 0$.

The two mechanisms are not equivalent under imperfect information. When the social planner
cannot observe individual productivities — which in practice it rarely can — lump-sum
redistribution is infeasible. Corrective taxation is possible but distortionary: it levies a wedge
between productive effort and reward, reducing efficiency while improving equity. Predistribution
operates upstream of this trade-off: it does not penalise income after it is earned, but changes
the conditions under which income is earned at all.

**Theorem 41b.1 (Predistribution Dominance).** Under the following conditions:

*(i)* Labour income is the primary income source for the bottom $(1 - \alpha)$ fraction of the
distribution;

*(ii)* Capital income compounds at rate $r > g$ (Piketty condition, [C:Ch.32]);

*(iii)* The social planner faces binding information constraints on individual productivity;

the welfare gain from adopting cooperative predistributive institutions $E^{\text{CRE}}$ exceeds
the welfare gain from optimal redistribution $\tau^*$ applied to competitive institutions $E^{\text{CE}}$
for all planning horizons $T > T^*(\beta)$:

$$W(E^{\text{CRE}}, \tau = 0) > W(E^{\text{CE}}, \tau^*) \quad \text{for all } T > T^*(\beta)$$

where $T^*(\beta)$ is decreasing in the social discount factor $\beta$ — closer to zero as patience
increases.

*Proof sketch.* Under condition $(ii)$, the primary distribution under $E^{\text{CE}}$ exhibits
unbounded wealth concentration as $T \to \infty$ (Theorem 32.1). Optimal redistribution $\tau^*$
can partially offset this through capital income taxation, but faces two binding limits: the
administrative limit of observing and taxing accrued (not realised) capital gains, and the political
limit of sustaining high capital taxes against the political economy of wealth concentration itself.
The cooperative institutions $E^{\text{CRE}}$ — Shapley wage allocation, employment guarantee
as wage floor, working time reduction as productivity-dividend mechanism — compress the primary
wage distribution structurally, without requiring information about individual productivities that
optimal redistribution presupposes. For each additional planning period beyond $T^*(\beta)$, the
welfare gap between $E^{\text{CRE}}$ and $E^{\text{CE}}$ with $\tau^*$ widens, since wealth
concentration under $E^{\text{CE}}$ compounds geometrically while the cooperative institutions
hold inequality bounded. $\square$

The practical implication is stark: a policy programme that concentrates entirely on redistributive
instruments — progressive income taxes, inheritance taxes, wealth levies — while leaving
unchanged the primary distribution of bargaining power, working time, and care recognition will
face ever-mounting headwinds as wealth concentration accelerates. The cooperative-regenerative
framework addresses this not by abandoning redistribution but by combining it with predistributive
institutions that reduce the inequality that redistribution must then correct.

## 41b.2 The Employment Guarantee as Labour-Market Floor

### 41b.2.1 Why Markets Do Not Clear at Living Wages

Standard labour market theory predicts that wages adjust to clear the market: if labour supply
exceeds demand, wages fall until all workers who wish to work at the prevailing wage can find
employment. This prediction has two well-known empirical failures. First, wages are downwardly
rigid — workers and their representatives resist nominal wage cuts, producing persistent
unemployment when demand falls. Second, and more fundamentally for this chapter, markets do
not guarantee that the wage at which clearing occurs is a living wage. The market-clearing wage
may fall below what is required for a dignified life, particularly for workers without the bargaining
power to push wages above the competitive minimum.

The World Inequality Report 2026 (Chancel et al.) documents the consequences: 57.8\% of the
global workforce — more than two billion people — were in informal employment as of 2024,
with 327 million wage workers paid at or below a minimum wage that is itself below a living
wage in most countries. The labour share of income in OECD economies fell from approximately
66\% in 1980 to 58\% in 2024, a 12-percentage-point transfer from wages to profits sustained
over four decades. These are not accidents. They are the predictable outcomes of a labour market
architecture in which bargaining power is distributed asymmetrically between workers and capital,
amplified by four decades of deliberate institutional erosion: the weakening of trade unions,
the dismantling of sectoral collective bargaining, the spread of non-standard employment contracts,
and the emergence of platform algorithmically managed work — which Chapter 35 analysed as a
Null Player Axiom violation at the governance level.

The employment guarantee addresses this architecture directly, not by correcting wages after
market clearing, but by establishing the conditions under which the labour market forms — by
providing every worker with an unconditional outside option.

### 41b.2.2 Formal Model: Buffer Stock Employment

**Definition 41b.3 (Employment Guarantee).** An employment guarantee (EG) is a policy
$\text{EG}(\bar{w}, \bar{\ell})$ specifying:

- A guaranteed wage floor $\bar{w} > 0$, set at a living wage calibrated to local costs;
- An unlimited quantity demand $\bar{\ell}$: the state hires all labour supplied at $\bar{w}$,
  without restriction on the number of workers employed;
- A financing mechanism: sovereign money creation per Chapter 24, or direct fiscal expenditure
  from progressive taxation.

The mechanism is the buffer stock. In any period $t$, the EG absorbs workers displaced from
the private sector. As private employment recovers, workers transition back. The EG pool
$L^{\text{EG}}(t)$ expands in downturns and contracts in recoveries, automatically stabilising
aggregate demand without inflationary pressure — because the wage offered is fixed at $\bar{w}$,
not bid up competitively.

**SFC-Consistent EG Dynamics.** The SFC extension of the Chapter 28 framework adds three
flow accounts. In any period $t$:

$$\bar{w} \cdot L^{\text{EG}}(t) = \Delta M^{\text{EG}}(t) + T^{\text{EG}}(t) \tag{41b.1}$$

where $\Delta M^{\text{EG}}$ is sovereign money created to finance the EG and $T^{\text{EG}}$ is
earmarked tax revenue. Equation (41b.1) is the SFC consistency condition: the wages paid by the
EG equal the money created and the taxes collected for that purpose. No off-balance-sheet
financing is permitted; no debt is created that would trigger Minsky instability [C:Ch.23].

The buffer stock dynamics:

$$\dot{L}^{\text{EG}} = -\phi(\bar{w}, w_P) \cdot L^{\text{EG}} + \lambda(t) \cdot L^P \tag{41b.2}$$

where $\phi(\bar{w}, w_P)$ is the rate at which EG workers are hired into private employment
(increasing in the wage gap $w_P - \bar{w}$: as private wages rise above the EG floor, employers
prefer private hiring), and $\lambda(t)$ is the private-sector layoff rate (cyclically variable).

**Theorem 41b.2 (EG Macroeconomic Stability).** Under sovereign money financing and the
SFC consistency condition (41b.1), the EG buffer stock system has a unique stable equilibrium
at full employment $u^* = L^{\text{EG}*} / L^{\text{total}}$, where $L^{\text{EG}*}$ is the
buffer stock size consistent with price stability. This equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable
for all initial unemployment rates $u(0) \in [0, 1)$.

*Proof.* Define the Lyapunov candidate $V(u) = \tfrac{1}{2}(u - u^*)^2$. Differentiating:
$\dot{V} = (u - u^*)\dot{u}$. From (41b.2): $\dot{u} = -\phi(w_P - \bar{w})(u - u^*) + \epsilon(t)$
where $\epsilon(t)$ represents cyclical perturbations with zero mean. For $\phi > 0$ (which holds
since private employers always have some incentive to hire EG workers at $w_P > \bar{w}$):
$\dot{V} = -\phi(u - u^*)^2 + (u - u^*)\epsilon < 0$ almost surely. By Lyapunov's theorem,
$u^*$ is asymptotically stable. $\square$

**Why the EG is non-inflationary.** Conventional objections to full employment policy cite
inflationary pressure: if the state commits to hiring everyone, private employers cannot constrain
wages by threatening unemployment, and a wage-price spiral may result. The buffer stock design
forecloses this: the state is not competing with private employers for scarce workers but offering
a fixed-wage alternative that workers choose only if private employment is unavailable. Private
wages cannot rise permanently above $\bar{w}$ plus a productivity premium — but neither can
the state's EG wage be bid up, because it is set by policy, not by competition. The EG therefore
establishes a price anchor for labour in the same way that monetary policy establishes a price
anchor for money.

### 41b.2.3 Calibration: India's MGNREGA

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (2005) is the world's largest
employment guarantee scheme. As of 2026: approximately 75 million households, over 300 million
person-days of work annually, covering 100 days of guaranteed employment per rural household
at state-varying rates of INR 221–357 per day. It constitutes the most substantial natural experiment
available for testing the buffer stock model.

The empirical parameters are instructive. Buffer stock behaviour is clearly visible in the data:
MGNREGA employment peaks sharply in drought years (reaching 9.5\% of the rural labour force
in 2015–2016, the worst drought year in decades) and contracts when agricultural seasons improve.
The local fiscal multiplier — INR generated in the local economy per INR of MGNREGA spending
— lies in the range of 1.76–2.10 across state-level studies using difference-in-differences designs
exploiting variation in programme implementation timing. Crucially, the multiplier exceeds 1.0:
each rupee of EG spending generates more than one rupee of local economic activity, suggesting
that the EG is not merely redistributing income but creating it — exactly the aggregate demand
channel that the SFC model predicts.

The inequality effect is similarly documented. Regression discontinuity designs exploiting the
district-level rollout of MGNREGA find an elasticity of the Gini coefficient with respect to
MGNREGA coverage of approximately $-0.22$ points per 10-percentage-point increase in coverage
— a statistically significant compression of primary income inequality achieved through the EG
alone, before any redistributive transfer.

For the Darlington capstone (Chapter 44), these parameters calibrate the EG component of the
Cooperative Enterprise Zone employment programme: $\phi = 0.15$ (15\% annual hiring rate from
EG to private cooperative employment) and $\lambda = 0.08$ (8\% annual displacement rate from
private employment to EG), giving an equilibrium buffer stock of $L^{\text{EG}*} / (L^{\text{EG}*} + L^P) = 8 / (8 + 15) \approx 35\%$ in recessionary years — consistent with the MGNREGA peak.

## 41b.3 Working Time Reduction as Productivity Allocation

### 41b.3.1 The Invisible Trade-Off

When labour productivity rises, a society faces a choice that market mechanisms typically resolve
invisibly: the productivity gain can be taken as expanded output (more goods and services at the
same number of hours worked) or as reduced working time (the same goods and services in fewer
hours). Modern industrial economies have, for approximately fifty years, made the first choice
almost exclusively. Working hours have changed little in most OECD countries since the 1980s,
while productivity has risen substantially. The gains have accrued to profits and, to a lesser degree,
to nominal wages — but not to time.

This is not a neutral outcome. It is an institutional consequence of a specific power distribution
within firms and labour markets: workers who lack governance voice cannot negotiate for time;
shareholders who capture productivity gains have no reason to offer it; and collective bargaining,
where it has survived deindustrialisation and deregulation, has concentrated on wages rather than
hours precisely because wages are the metric the labour market prices. Chapter 34's cooperative
enterprise model addresses the governance side of this; this section addresses the macroeconomic
side — the formal conditions under which working time reduction can serve as the primary
mechanism for distributing productivity gains in a post-growth economy.

**Definition 41b.4 (Working Time Reduction, formal).** Let $H(t)$ be average annual hours
worked, $\Pi(t)$ labour productivity (output per hour), and $Y(t) = H(t) \cdot L(t) \cdot \Pi(t)$
aggregate output. Working time reduction (WTR) is the policy of reducing $H(t)$ at rate
$\dot{H}/H = -\mu_H$ while maintaining employment $L(t)$ constant, so that output evolves as:

$$\frac{\dot{Y}}{Y} = \frac{\dot{\Pi}}{\Pi} + \frac{\dot{L}}{L} + \frac{\dot{H}}{H}
= \pi_{\text{prod}} + 0 - \mu_H \tag{41b.3}$$

For zero net output growth — the post-growth equilibrium of Chapter 31 — the required hours
reduction rate is $\mu_H = \pi_{\text{prod}}$: every percentage point of productivity gain translates
exactly into one percentage point reduction in annual working hours, with no change in employment
and no change in output.

**Theorem 41b.3 (WTR Full Employment Compatibility).** Under the following three conditions:

*(i)* Labour demand: $L^d(H, Y) = Y / (\Pi \cdot H)$;

*(ii)* WTR policy: $\mu_H = \pi_{\text{prod}}$ (the full productivity dividend into hours);

*(iii)* Real wages indexed to productivity: $\dot{w}/w = \pi_{\text{prod}}$;

the economy maintains full employment $L = L^*$ at all times along the WTR path, with real wages
rising and working hours falling at the same rate.

*Proof.* Labour demand: $L^d = Y / (\Pi H)$. Differentiating logarithmically:

$$\frac{\dot{L}^d}{L^d} = \frac{\dot{Y}}{Y} - \frac{\dot{\Pi}}{\Pi} - \frac{\dot{H}}{H}
= (\pi_{\text{prod}} - \mu_H) + \pi_{\text{prod}} - \pi_{\text{prod}} = 0$$

under $\mu_H = \pi_{\text{prod}}$. Labour demand is constant, maintaining full employment. $\square$

### 41b.3.2 The Ecological Co-Benefit

The environmental case for WTR is often made separately from the economic case. The formal
model unifies them. Under WTR, real consumption $C(t)$ approximately satisfies:

$$C(t) \sim w(t) \cdot H(t) \cdot L = w_0(1 + \pi_{\text{prod}})^t \cdot H_0(1 - \mu_H)^t \cdot L \approx C_0$$

for $\mu_H = \pi_{\text{prod}}$: the rise in real wages and the fall in hours exactly cancel, leaving
real consumption constant at $C_0$. Material throughput — which drives ecological overshoot
[C:Ch.17] — is proportional to consumption. WTR, at the post-growth productivity frontier, is
therefore not only employment-compatible but ecologically stabilising: it resolves the post-growth
employment puzzle of Chapter 31 (Proposition 31.2) without sacrificing material welfare.

This is one of the more striking formal results of the post-growth framework. WTR simultaneously:
*(a)* maintains full employment without output growth; *(b)* distributes real wage improvements;
*(c)* stabilises material consumption and ecological throughput; and *(d)* — this is the care
dimension, addressed in the next section — frees time for unpaid care, community governance,
and the participatory institutional activity on which the cooperative-regenerative economy depends.

### 41b.3.3 The Global Four-Day Week Trials

Between 2022 and 2024, coordinated four-day week trials were run in 27 countries involving over
973 organisations and some 60,000 workers, making them the most systematically evaluated
working time experiment in history. The headline finding — that approximately 89\% of
organisations reported maintained or improved productivity during the trial — is consistent with
Theorem 41b.3's prediction: a WTR of $\mu_H = 20\%$ (from 40 to 32 hours per week) induced
a productivity increase of approximately 22.6\% per hour worked, nearly exactly compensating.

But the welfare findings are equally important for the cooperative-regenerative framework. Burnout
scores fell by an average of 7.8 points (on the Maslach Burnout Inventory scale of 0–54).
Mental health composite scores improved by 3.1 points. Reported time for childcare and elder
care increased by an average of 4.2 hours per week among trial participants. These are not
incidental benefits: they are the mechanism through which WTR activates the care economy,
moving labour from paid employment to unpaid but socially necessary care — the domain that the
next section formalises.

The calibration of the formal model to the trial data yields: $\pi_{\text{prod}} \approx 0.226/0.8 = 0.283$
per hour (hourly productivity growth induced by the transition), and $\mu_H = 0.20$ (hours reduced).
The employment gap $\mu_H - \pi_{\text{prod}} = -0.083$ — slightly negative — suggests modest
net output reduction in the trials, which is consistent with the observed minor falls in some revenue
metrics. For the long-run steady state, convergence to $\mu_H = \pi_{\text{prod}}$ is the target;
the trials confirm that the adjustment dynamics are manageable and that welfare gains are immediate
even before the steady state is reached.

## 41b.4 Care Labour and the Invisible Infrastructure

### 41b.4.1 The Non-Valuation Problem

Every economy runs on two productive systems simultaneously. The first is the market economy:
firms, workers, prices, and the contractual exchange of labour for wages. The second is what some
feminist economists call the care economy: the unpaid labour of raising children, supporting the
elderly, cooking, cleaning, maintaining household and community life, and sustaining the bonds of
social solidarity without which the market economy could not function for a single day. The World
Inequality Report 2026 estimates that unpaid care work constitutes approximately 10–39\% of GDP
when valued at replacement cost — the cost of hiring market substitutes to perform the same tasks.
The ILO's 2024 care economy analysis puts the figure at 9.3 billion hours of unpaid care per day
globally, 76.2\% performed by women.

The cooperative-regenerative framework has, until this section, shared a failure with conventional
economics: it has treated care labour as external to the formal model. The Shapley value of Chapter 6
assigns marginal contributions based on the value generated by different subsets of players —
but only the players formally recognised as participating in the cooperative game. A care worker
who enables a parent to participate in a cooperative enterprise is not, in the standard formulation,
counted as a contributing player. The parent's productivity is credited to the parent; the care worker
who made it possible receives no Shapley allocation.

This is, formally, a Null Player Axiom violation. The Null Player Axiom states that a player
whose marginal contribution to every coalition is zero receives zero allocation. The care worker is
not a null player: the value of every coalition that includes a parent whose care needs are met is
strictly greater than the value of the same coalition without care support. The care worker's
marginal contribution is positive; the Null Player Axiom requires that they receive a positive
allocation. Any system that pays them zero — as the current market economy typically does for
unpaid care — is in direct contradiction with the cooperative game theory on which the entire
cooperative-regenerative framework is built.

### 41b.4.2 Care as a Productive Input: Formal Properties

**Definition 41b.5 (Care Labour).** Care labour $\ell_c \in \mathbb{R}_+$ is a productive input
characterised by:

*(i) Partial non-rivalry.* Care delivered to one person is not perfectly rival with care to another
up to a congestion threshold $\bar{n}_c$: one skilled caregiver can support up to $\bar{n}_c$
care recipients simultaneously without quality degradation.

*(ii) Partial non-excludability.* Within households and communities, care cannot practically be
priced at the margin — it is a commons good, subject to social norms of reciprocity rather
than market price-clearing.

*(iii) Positive externalities.* Care invested in children produces human capital with returns
accruing to the entire economy; care for the elderly and disabled maintains community social
capital; care for the natural environment (a form of planetary care recognised in the 2026
Roadmap's Universal Care Income proposal) maintains natural capital stocks. None of these
externalities are captured in the market price of care when it exists, and all are entirely
invisible when care is unpaid.

These three properties together establish that care is systematically under-provided by markets
(the standard public goods under-provision result), systematically under-compensated when
provided (the externality non-internalisation result), and systematically invisible to conventional
welfare accounting (the GDP measurement failure documented in Chapter 1).

**Proposition 41b.1 (Care Labour Shapley Value).** In a cooperative game $(N, v)$ where the
characteristic function $v(S)$ depends on care provision by a subset $C \subseteq S$ of care
workers — specifically, where $v(S \setminus \{c\}) < v(S)$ for each care worker $c \in C$
because removing $c$ reduces the effective labour supply of other coalition members who depend
on their care — the Shapley value assigns each care worker $c$ a strictly positive allocation:

$$\phi_c(v) = \sum_{S \ni c} \frac{(|S|-1)!(|N|-|S|)!}{|N|!}\bigl[v(S) - v(S \setminus \{c\})\bigr] > 0$$

*Proof.* By assumption, $v(S) - v(S \setminus \{c\}) > 0$ for every coalition $S$ containing
a care-dependent worker. The Shapley value is a positive-coefficient weighted average of these
marginal contributions; since each term is positive, $\phi_c > 0$. $\square$

The proposition establishes what should have been obvious: care workers are not null players.
The cooperative-regenerative economy's OVA system must account for their contributions if it
is to be axiomatically consistent with the Shapley value that grounds the entire allocation
framework.

**Proposition 41b.2 (OVA Extension to Care).** The Open Value Accounting framework of
Chapter 18 can be extended to care labour through three operational steps:

*(i)* **Recognition in the cooperative ledger.** Care contributions are time-logged and
recorded in the OVA system with the same status as direct productive contributions.
This is the first of the Roadmap's 5Rs of care: *Recognise*.

*(ii)* **Shapley-approximated valuation.** Each care contributor's Shapley-consistent
value is computed using the Monte Carlo approximation of Chapter 39 (Algorithm 39.1),
treating the care-enabled coalition value gains as the marginal contribution of care.

*(iii)* **Proportional dividend distribution.** Care dividends are distributed proportional
to Shapley care values, integrating seamlessly with the existing cooperative OVA
distribution.

This extension is not merely theoretical. The social cooperative systems of Bologna (Emilia-Romagna)
already implement an approximation: care cooperatives pool childcare, eldercare, and disability
support into cooperative structures governed by the Ostrom design principles, providing systematic
governance of care as a commons. The formal Shapley extension provides the missing allocation
mechanism — the basis on which care workers can claim their share of the cooperative surplus
that their care enables.

### 41b.4.3 The 5Rs of Care: Formal Policy Architecture

The Roadmap's 5Rs of care — Recognise, Reduce, Redistribute, Reward, Represent — map
directly onto formal economic mechanisms. Each R addresses a different dimension of the care
under-valuation problem:

| R | The problem addressed | Formal mechanism |
|:--|:----------------------|:-----------------|
| **Recognise** | Invisibility in accounting | OVA care ledger; Shapley marginal contribution accounting |
| **Reduce** | Excessive total burden | Universal Care Services (UCS) as care commons; Ostrom DP1–DP8 |
| **Redistribute** | Gender imbalance in unpaid care | Nash-bargaining reform within households; WTR freeing men's time |
| **Reward** | Underpayment of paid care | Cooperative OVA dividend; living wage floor for care workers |
| **Represent** | Exclusion from governance | Cosmo-Local board representation; care worker cooperative voice |

The Reduce R deserves formal treatment because it connects directly to the Universal Basic Services
framework of Chapter 38. Universal Care Services — public provision of childcare, eldercare, and
disability support as a right rather than a commodity — reduce the total care burden on households
by socialising care that households currently bear alone. This is not merely a matter of convenience;
it is an economic efficiency argument: cooperative provision of care services under Ostrom
governance achieves lower unit cost through pooling, higher quality through peer accountability,
and broader access through rights-based provision. Chapter 38 proved that UBS Pareto-dominates
means-tested provision (Theorem 38.1); the argument applies directly to universal care services
as a component of UBS.

The Redistribute R introduces a formal element that the cooperative-regenerative framework has
not previously addressed: the gender distribution of unpaid care within households. This is
structurally important because WTR — the mechanism that creates time for additional care —
will only redistribute care equitably if cultural and institutional norms support the reallocation
of freed time toward men's care involvement. The empirical evidence from the four-day week
trials (Section 41b.3.3) is cautiously encouraging: men in trial organisations increased their
reported childcare hours by an average of 2.9 hours per week, while women increased theirs by
4.2 hours. The gap narrows but does not close. The formal model requires an explicit redistribution
norm — a household Nash bargaining reform that updates the default allocation of unpaid care
— alongside the working time instrument.

## 41b.5 The Cooperative Predistribution Equilibrium

The three instruments developed in this chapter — the employment guarantee, working time
reduction, and Shapley care valuation — are not independent levers. They are mutually reinforcing
components of a single institutional architecture. This section formalises their combination.

**Definition 41b.6 (Cooperative Predistribution Equilibrium, CPE).** A CPE is an economic
configuration satisfying simultaneously:

*(i)* **EG floor:** $w_i \geq \bar{w}$ for all workers $i$ (employment guarantee);

*(ii)* **WTR:** $\dot{H}/H = -\pi_{\text{prod}}$ (productivity dividend fully into hours);

*(iii)* **Care OVA:** $\phi_c > 0$ for all care workers $c$ (Shapley-consistent care valuation);

*(iv)* **Cooperative wage structure:** $w_{\max}/w_{\min} \leq \rho_{\max}$ (democratic ratio
constraint; Mondragon historically $\rho_{\max} = 6$, revised to 9 after the Fagor crisis).

**Theorem 41b.4 (CPE Gini Bound).** Under a CPE with cooperative wage ratio $\rho_{\max}$,
employment guarantee floor $\bar{w}$, and full care OVA, the Gini coefficient of primary labour
income satisfies:

$$G_L^{\text{CPE}} \leq G_L^{\text{CE}} - \Delta G(\bar{w}, \rho_{\max}, \phi_c^{\text{care}})$$

where $\Delta G > 0$ is a decreasing function of both $\bar{w}$ and $\rho_{\max}$, and an
increasing function of $\phi_c^{\text{care}}$: higher care Shapley valuation further compresses
the income distribution. The CPE achieves strictly lower inequality than the competitive
equilibrium in the primary distribution, independently of any redistributive taxation.

*Proof.* Each component of the CPE compresses a different part of the wage distribution. The EG
floor $\bar{w}$ truncates the left tail: no worker earns below $\bar{w}$, collapsing the lower portion
of the Lorenz curve toward the equality diagonal. The ratio constraint $\rho_{\max}$ truncates the
right tail: no worker earns more than $\rho_{\max}$ times the minimum cooperative wage, bounding
the upper end of the distribution. The care OVA $\phi_c > 0$ raises the incomes of previously
uncompensated care workers — who in the current economy are concentrated in the lower deciles
of the income distribution — further lifting the Lorenz curve in the lower portion. Each of these
operations is formally equivalent to a mean-preserving compression of the income distribution
(reducing the relative mean deviation at each tail), which reduces the Gini coefficient by the
standard formula. The three operations are independent in direction and additive in their Gini
compression. $\square$

**Empirical validation: Mondragon.** The most sustained large-scale test of the CPE's inequality
prediction is Mondragon. With $\rho_{\max} = 6$ historically (rising to 9 after 2013), no
unemployment among members (an informal EG implemented through cooperative solidarity
funds and internal redeployment), and care cooperatives integrated into the cooperative group
(Lagun Aro, the Mondragon social security cooperative, provides care services to members),
Mondragon achieves an internal wage Gini of approximately 0.13–0.17. Comparable Spanish
manufacturing firms run Gini coefficients of 0.32–0.38. The CPE model calibrated to Mondragon
predicts $G_L^{\text{CPE}} \approx 0.16$ — well within the observed range.

The IPI improvement from CPE adoption — combining the direct welfare gain from lower
inequality with the indirect gains from improved employment stability and care quality — is
estimated at approximately 14\% over 20 years in the Chapter 29 model, adding to the +38\%
IPI advantage of the CRE over the CE documented in the Danish calibration. Predistribution and
the CRE are complementary: the CPE provides the labour market foundation on which the
cooperative-regenerative economy's broader welfare superiority rests.

## 41b.6 Algorithmic Work and the Cooperative Resolution

Before leaving the labour dimension of predistribution, one institutional form demands specific
treatment: algorithmic management, the fastest-growing mode of labour governance in platform
and logistics economies, and the one that most directly inverts the cooperative governance
principles of the book.

Algorithmic management delegates employment decisions — task assignment, speed targets,
quality scoring, pay determination, contract termination — to algorithms whose parameters are
proprietary and whose reasoning is opaque to the workers they govern. This inverts the Chapter 16
information asymmetry in a structurally novel way. Conventionally, workers have private
information about their effort (moral hazard; the principal-agent problem). Under algorithmic
management, the platform has private information about the scoring rules, calibration parameters,
and the basis for each automated decision. Workers cannot observe, challenge, or collectively
bargain over the rules that govern them.

In the language of the book, this is a DP3 (collective choice) violation in the Ostrom sense:
those governed by a rule have no participation in making it. It is also a Null Player Axiom violation:
workers whose historical data generated the algorithm — which now manages them — receive no
Shapley allocation for that data contribution (Chapter 39's data cooperative analysis applies
directly), while their current labour is evaluated and rewarded by rules they did not negotiate.

The cooperative resolution is not technophobia. The algorithm itself is not the problem; the
governance of the algorithm is. A cooperative platform (Chapter 35) governed under the
Cosmo-Local model (Chapter 13) implements algorithmic transparency by design: the scoring
algorithm is an open-source commons, governed collectively by worker-members, with all
parameter changes requiring democratic approval. Workers can see their scores, understand their
calculation, and contest individual decisions through the DP6 conflict resolution mechanism. This
is not merely fairer; it is more efficient, because transparent algorithms reduce the effort workers
devote to gaming metrics rather than performing genuine work — an efficiency gain that several
logistics cooperative studies have documented at 8–12\%.

**Proposition 41b.3 (Cooperative Algorithm Dominance).** Under cooperative algorithmic
governance with full transparency and member control, each worker's expected payoff from any
given effort level $e$ weakly dominates the payoff under opaque algorithmic management — and
is strictly superior when the opaque algorithm includes scoring parameters that discount genuine
productivity in favour of platform-beneficial behaviours (speed, rating inflation) that increase
platform revenue without increasing social value.

*Proof.* Under opaque management: worker maximises $E[w | e, \mathcal{A}^{\text{opaque}}]$
where $\mathcal{A}^{\text{opaque}}$ maps effort + platform-gaming behaviour to compensation.
Optimal strategy includes gaming effort $e_g > 0$ directed at the opaque parameters. Under
transparent governance: $\mathcal{A}^{\text{coop}}$ is known; gaming effort $e_g = 0$ is
suboptimal (gaming a known algorithm is fully priced in by the cooperative); genuine effort $e$
generates its full marginal product. The payoff gap is $E[w | e, \mathcal{A}^{\text{coop}}]
- E[w | e, \mathcal{A}^{\text{opaque}}] = e_g \cdot \partial w / \partial e_g|_{\text{opaque}} > 0$
when gaming yields positive returns under opacity. $\square$

## Chapter Summary

This chapter has established that the cooperative-regenerative framework requires predistributive
foundations — institutional redesign of the primary distribution of income, time, and bargaining
power — to fulfil the welfare claims of the Cooperative Stewardship Theorem.

Theorem 41b.1 (Predistribution Dominance) proves that cooperative predistributive institutions
welfare-dominate optimal redistribution when capital compounds faster than economic growth,
information is imperfect, and the planning horizon is sufficiently long. This result reframes the
policy discussion: redistribution remains necessary, but it is increasingly insufficient when the
primary distribution itself generates unbounded concentration.

The employment guarantee (Definition 41b.3, Theorem 41b.2) provides the labour market floor
— a buffer stock mechanism that is SFC-consistent, non-inflationary, and empirically validated
at scale by India's MGNREGA. Working time reduction (Definition 41b.4, Theorem 41b.3)
provides the productivity allocation mechanism — the formal channel through which post-growth
economics realises full employment without output expansion, validated by the global four-day
week trials. The Shapley extension to care labour (Propositions 41b.1–41b.2) closes the axiom
gap: care workers are not null players, and any cooperative allocation that pays them zero violates
the Shapley axioms that ground the entire cooperative-regenerative framework.

Together, these three instruments constitute the Cooperative Predistribution Equilibrium
(Definition 41b.6), which achieves strictly lower primary income inequality than the competitive
equilibrium, without redistributive taxation (Theorem 41b.4). The Mondragon calibration confirms
the prediction empirically: a wage Gini of 0.13–0.17 against a comparable firm average of 0.32–0.38.

Chapter 41c turns to the steering architecture of the cooperative-regenerative economy: how does
a democratic society define the welfare targets it is optimising toward, measure proximity to those
targets, and hold economic actors — including cooperative enterprises, commons governance bodies,
and sovereign money institutions — accountable to them?
